## And up I Flew - to Mexico
The flight from Boston to Mexico was long and thankfully
uneventful. I slept on and off and rummaged in the seat pocket in front
where I found a fold out card with a blue ribbon on the front. It was
not the familiar list of safety instructions but additional information
on a more discreet emergency, human trafficking is big North-
South business. A series of line drawings cut through the difficulties
of language, and I began my journey to Latin America ‘reading’ the
‘tableau style’ image, a catalogue of human behaviour to look out
for clear visual signs of a distressed woman being coerced for the
financial benefit of others and the numerous individuals willing to pay.
Visually, the simplified drawings reminded me of the 1641 Depositions
held at Trinity College Dublin, basic images depicting horrific war
stories. I slipped the laminated brochure back into the seat pocket and
wondered if anyone passing through a busy airport would ever report
seeing anything. I felt a tinge of despair that in 2024 international
slavery and its supply and demand continuum remains part of the
human experience.

## How did the English arrive in Ireland?
English involvement in Ireland began in the twelfth century
when a local ruler, Dermot MacMurrough, invited Norman forces

eighteenth century, the Penal Laws severely restricted Catholics,
barring them from owning land or freely practicing their faith. The

## The Easter Rising
By the early twentieth century, the nationalist movement had
gained momentum. The Home Rule movement succeeded in passing
legislation to restore the Irish parliament in 1914, but World War I
delayed its implementation, frustrating many nationalists.
Determined to act, the IRB, along with the Irish Volunteers and

forces suppressed the uprising with overwhelming force, devastating
central Dublin. Approximately 429 people were killed, including 310
civilians. Sixteen leaders were executed for treason, and many others

## The Silent Era and Early Productions (1890–1920)
Cinema arrived in Ireland shortly after its first screenings in
Paris and London, as early as the 1890s. Screenings were conducted
by traveling exhibitors who set up screens in villages and towns,
utilizing venues such as concert halls and theaters (Rockett, Gibbons,
Hill, 1988). It was only in 1909 that the first permanent cinema, the
Volta, opened in Dublin, spearheaded by James Joyce and a group
of investors.
During this period, most films shown were foreign, with few local
productions. Notable exceptions include:
- Life on the Great Southern and Western Railway (1904), a
documentary by Louis de Clerq, considered the first Irish film;
- A Cattle Drive in Galway (1908) and Whaling Ashore and
Afloat (1908), by Robert Paul, depicting rural and coastal
scenes;
- Glimpses of Erin (1910), by Arthur Melbourne-Cooper, a series
of short films showcasing Irish landscapes and customs.
- The Film Company of Ireland (FCOI), founded in 1916,
represented the first attempt to establish a national film
industry. However, its early projects were lost during the
Easter Rising when the company’s offices were destroyed.

## The Three Waves of Contemporary Cinema
The three waves of contemporary Irish cinema are not rigidly
defined by dates but by thematic shifts reflecting historical moments:
- The First Wave (1970s–1980s) emerged with the consolidation
of the Irish Film Board (1983–1987) and was characterized
by avant-garde filmmaking.
- The Second Wave (1990s–2000s), known as the “Celtic
Tiger” era, began around 1993 with the re-establishment
of the IFB and was marked by economic growth and
technological advancements.
- The Third Wave (Post-2000s), or “Post-Celtic Tiger” cinema,
addressed the consequences of rapid economic expansion
and subsequent recession, peaking around 2006.

Contemporary Irish cinema has become a critical space

Ireland and Brazil: a cultural dialogue is the result of a collaborative project
between the Alliance of Chairs of Irish Studies in Latin America, based at the
National University of La Pampa (UNLPam), Argentina, the National Autonomous
University of Mexico (UNAM) and the University of São Paulo (USP). The
volume opens with an account of Irish artist Rita Duffy’s trip to the three countries
as part of the LAC Guest Professor Programme of the Irish Department of Foreign
Affairs. She describes her sensory impressions of nature and the pulsating life
of the cities where she engaged in artistic work. Divided into five chapters, the
book takes the reader on a journey that highlights the crossing paths of enduring
historical connections between Brazil and Ireland by exploring its literature,
theatre, cinema, visual arts and culture, as well as tackling topics such as sports
and scientific contributions.
Ireland and Mexico: A Cultural Dialogue is a volume that brings together
chronicles and articles concerning the long-standing and deeply complex cultural
relationship between Ireland and Mexico. Diverse viewpoints from artists and
experts converge in these pages to offer, with briskness and insight, a journey
through the paths our two nations have travelled over several centuries of shared
history.
Ireland and Argentina: A Cultural Dialogue introduces a variety of voices
and topics: literature and arts, history, politics, science, education and sports, as
a steppingstone for those interested in learning about the ongoing relationships
between Ireland and Argentina along centuries of intermingled histories.
